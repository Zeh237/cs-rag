CHAPTER ONE: INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background

Universities generate and manage large volumes of academic material. Lecture notes, research papers, theses, and course handouts form the core of teaching and learning. At the University of Buea, as in many institutions, these resources are distributed across departments, stored in different formats, and often accessed through fragmented channels.

Digital libraries offer a central place to store and manage such collections. A well-designed digital library improves access, preserves knowledge, and supports research. However, traditional digital libraries rely on keyword-based search. Keyword search matches exact terms but does not understand meaning. It struggles with paraphrased questions, broad topics, and conceptual queries.

Recent advances in Natural Language Processing have introduced new possibilities. Large Language Models can generate fluent and context-aware answers. Retrieval-Augmented Generation combines the strengths of search and generation. It first retrieves relevant passages from a trusted collection and then uses a language model to formulate an answer grounded in those passages. This approach reduces hallucination and provides verifiable citations.

This project presents a general-purpose digital library system built on Retrieval-Augmented Generation. While the system is designed to house materials from any department, this work uses Computer Science notes as a representative case study to develop, implement, and evaluate the core pipeline.

1.2 Problem Statement

Students and staff at the University of Buea face three main challenges when accessing academic materials:

First, information is fragmented. Notes are shared through different platforms and in different formats, primarily PDF, which makes unified search difficult.

Second, existing search tools are limited. Keyword search fails when a query uses different wording from the source document. It also cannot synthesize information from multiple documents to answer broad conceptual questions.

Third, generative AI tools, while powerful, are not grounded in institutional content. They can produce plausible but incorrect answers and do not cite verifiable institutional sources.

There is a need for a unified, intelligent library system that can ingest heterogeneous academic documents, understand their content semantically, and provide accurate, citable answers to natural language questions.

1.3 Motivation

A central, intelligent digital library will directly support the academic mission of the University of Buea. It will help students revise more effectively, assist researchers in discovering relevant material, and provide staff with a reliable way to manage and disseminate knowledge.

The choice of Computer Science notes for this initial implementation is deliberate. The material is technical, structured, and conceptually dense, containing definitions, algorithms, code, and mathematical notation. If the system can accurately handle this complexity, it can be confidently extended to other disciplines with more narrative content.

1.4 Aim and Objectives

Aim:
To design, implement, and evaluate a scalable Retrieval-Augmented Generation system for a university digital library.

Specific Objectives:
1. To design a general ingestion pipeline that can extract clean text from PDF, Markdown, and text documents and preserve structural information such as headings and page numbers.
2. To implement and compare multiple text extraction methods, including Vision Language Models and traditional PDF parsing techniques.
3. To implement and compare five chunking strategies (fixed, recursive, semantic, document, and hierarchical) for splitting documents into searchable units.
4. To implement dense, sparse, and hybrid retrieval using vector embeddings and Elasticsearch, with support for reranking.
5. To develop a web-based interface for document ingestion, semantic search, conversational question answering, and source-grounded PDF viewing.
6. To evaluate the system on extraction quality, chunking integrity, and retrieval effectiveness using standard metrics such as Precision, Recall, Mean Average Precision, Mean Reciprocal Rank, and Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain.

1.5 Scope of the Project

The project covers the full pipeline from document ingestion to answer generation. It includes PDF-to-Markdown conversion, text cleaning, chunking, embedding, indexing, retrieval, and generation. The evaluation is conducted on a collection of undergraduate Computer Science notes. The system is built as a general framework; the Computer Science collection serves as a testbed and does not limit the system to that domain. The project does not cover large-scale user deployment or integration with the university's existing student information system, which are identified as future work.

1.6 Significance of the Study

This study contributes in three ways. First, it provides the University of Buea with a blueprint and a working prototype for a modern, AI-enabled digital library that can be extended to all faculties. Second, it provides an empirical comparison of text extraction and chunking strategies, offering practical guidance on how to prepare technical documents for retrieval. Third, it demonstrates how to combine sparse and dense retrieval to achieve both precise term matching and semantic understanding while maintaining full traceability to source pages.

1.7 Methodology Overview

The methodology follows a design, implementation, and evaluation cycle. The system is implemented in Python using Elasticsearch for storage and search, sentence-transformers for embeddings, and a local Large Language Model for generation. Document processing reuses established libraries such as LangChain and LlamaIndex for chunking. Evaluation is performed through automated scripts that measure structural integrity of chunks (coverage, sentence splitting) and retrieval quality on a synthetic question-answering dataset.

1.8 Organization of the Thesis

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows: Chapter Two reviews related work on digital libraries, text extraction, chunking, and retrieval-augmented generation. Chapter Three describes the system design and architecture. Chapter Four details implementation. Chapter Five presents evaluation and results. Chapter Six concludes the work and outlines directions for future research.
